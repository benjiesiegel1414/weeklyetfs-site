The quick answer

The main investments that pay weekly are weekly distribution ETFs. They come in three flavors: single-stock option income funds (like $NVDY, $MSTY or Roundhill's WeeklyPay funds such as $PLTW), index option income funds (like $XDTE and $QDTE), and one weekly Treasury bill fund, $WEEK. Regular stocks, bonds, CDs and savings accounts don't pay weekly.

The catch: high weekly yields usually come with price decay. Of the roughly 110 weekly payers on the WeeklyETFs.com list, only 4 have no price decay flag, and about 4 in 10 show a negative total return since launch. Weekly is a payment schedule, not a quality score.

Why almost nothing pays weekly

Most income investments are built around slow cash flow. Companies earn money over a quarter and pay a dividend once a quarter. Bonds usually pay interest twice a year. Savings accounts and CDs usually credit interest monthly. Money market funds earn interest every day, but most pay it out once a month.

Weekly ETFs flip that. Most of them sell options that expire every week, or even every day, so fresh premium comes in constantly. Paying it out weekly is a design choice, and it's a good marketing hook. Here's the part most people miss: a weekly check isn't more money. $1,000 a year paid weekly is still $1,000 a year. What changes is timing and, sometimes, how fast you can reinvest.

I covered the stock side in detail in Weekly Dividend Stocks 2026. Short version: there are practically none. If you want weekly income, you're looking at ETFs.

1. The safest weekly payer: a T-bill ETF

$WEEK, the Roundhill Weekly T-Bill ETF, holds nothing but U.S. Treasury bills maturing in 0 to 3 months and pays the interest out every week. Roundhill listed a 3.73% distribution rate as of October 30, 2026, and the share price has stayed right around $100. It's one of only four weekly payers on our list with no price decay flag.

$10,000 in $WEEK pays roughly $7 a week. That's not exciting, and it isn't supposed to be. It's the weekly version of parking cash in T-bills, and the payout falls when short-term rates fall. Full breakdown in my $WEEK dividend, yield and holdings guide.

2. Index option income funds that pay weekly

These sell options on a whole index instead of one stock. Roundhill's $XDTE (S&P 500), $QDTE (Nasdaq-100) and $RDTE (Russell 2000) sell options that expire the same day, every trading day, and pay out weekly. On our list $XDTE yields about 13%, $QDTE about 28% and $RDTE about 16%. Since launch, our list shows total returns of about 46% for $XDTE, 55% for $QDTE and 39% for $RDTE.

I like this group more than the single-stock funds because one bad earnings report can't wreck the whole fund. But they still flag price decay on our list. Selling options caps the upside, so in a strong rally you collect premium while the index runs away from you.

3. Single-stock weekly ETFs (where the huge yields are)

This is most of the list. YieldMax, Roundhill WeeklyPay, GraniteShares YieldBOOST, REX and others each run funds tied to one stock: $NVDA, $TSLA, $PLTR, $COIN, $MSTR and dozens more. The yields are eye-popping. $MSTW shows about 101% on our list, $PLTW about 78%, $MSTY about 60%.

Here's what I actually look at instead of the yield: total return since launch. $NVDY shows about 335% on our list because it rode a historic $NVDA run. $AMDW shows about 245%. But $MSTW shows about -79%, $AIYY about -75% and $COIW about -60%. Same structure, wildly different outcomes. The checks kept coming in all of them. The principal didn't. If you want to see what one of these looks like payout by payout, my sister site has the full $NVDY dividend history for 2026.

Roundhill's WeeklyPay funds are a little different: they use leverage (about 1.2x the stock's weekly return) and pay out every week. I broke down how they work in Best WeeklyPay ETFs to Buy in 2026.

Only 4 weekly payers on our list have no price decay flag: $WEEK, $AMDW, $CHPY and $FIZY. That's 4 out of about 110. See them all ranked in Weekly ETFs With the Lowest Price Decay.

4. The DIY option: build a weekly paycheck from monthly payers

You don't need a weekly ETF to get paid weekly. You can own four monthly payers that pay in different weeks of the month. For example, JPMorgan's $JEPI and $JEPQ, NEOS funds like $SPYI and $QQQI, and T-bill funds each run on their own monthly calendars. Line up four with different pay weeks and you've got cash arriving roughly every week.

I like this approach for the core of an income portfolio because you can pick funds with long track records and no price decay, and still get the weekly rhythm. The tradeoff is more work: you have to check each fund's calendar. MonthlyETFs.com lists the monthly payers, and TopDividendETFs.com has the dividend dates for $JEPQ, $SPYI, $QQQI and more.

The risks of weekly income investments

What can go wrong: Most weekly ETFs sell options, which caps upside and leaves you with the downside. Payouts change every week and can shrink fast when prices fall or volatility cools. Many distributions are estimated to be return of capital, which can mean part of your "income" is your own money. Single-stock funds can lose most of their value. Many of these funds are young, with short track records. And weekly checks can make it tempting to spend everything instead of reinvesting.

None of that means weekly ETFs are bad. It means you judge them on total return, not on the yield or the schedule. If you want to see that math fund by fund, ETFTotalReturns.com is built for it.

My takeaway

If you want investments that pay weekly, you're really choosing between weekly ETFs: a T-bill fund like $WEEK for safety, index option income funds like $XDTE and $QDTE for diversified high income, and single-stock funds for the big yields and the big risks. Or you can build a weekly rhythm yourself from monthly payers.

Here's how I'd think about it. Put the money that can't take a hit somewhere steady. Use diversified funds for the core of your weekly income. Keep the single-stock funds small, and only if you'd be fine owning the underlying stock anyway. And check total return every quarter, because a fund can pay you every Friday and still lose you money. Investments that pay weekly: questions, answered

What investments pay weekly?

The main investments that pay weekly are weekly distribution ETFs: single-stock option income funds like $NVDY, $MSTY and Roundhill WeeklyPay ETFs, index option income funds like $XDTE and $QDTE, and the Roundhill Weekly T-Bill ETF ($WEEK). Regular stocks, bonds, CDs and savings accounts generally do not pay weekly.

Are there any stocks that pay weekly dividends?

Practically none. U.S. companies usually pay dividends quarterly, and some REITs and business development companies pay monthly. To get paid every week you generally need a weekly paying ETF, or several monthly payers with different pay dates.

What is the safest investment that pays weekly?

Among weekly payers, a Treasury bill fund like $WEEK is the most conservative. It holds only U.S. Treasury bills maturing in 0 to 3 months, had a 3.73% distribution rate as of October 30, 2026, and is one of only four weekly payers on the WeeklyETFs.com list with no price decay flag. It is not FDIC insured and its payout falls when rates fall.

Do any ETFs pay dividends daily?

Not that we know of. Some ETFs, like $XDTE and $QDTE, sell options that expire every trading day, but they pay distributions weekly. Money market funds accrue interest daily but typically pay it monthly.

How much money do I need to make $100 a week from investments?

$100 a week is $5,200 a year. At a 3.7% yield you would need about $140,500, at 13% about $40,000, and at 40% about $13,000. Higher yields usually come with more price decay, so the smaller amount can shrink over time. This is not financial advice.
