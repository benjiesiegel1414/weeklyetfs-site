What is $MSFW?

$MSFW is the Roundhill MSFT WeeklyPay ETF. It started trading on July 24, 2025, and it tries to give you 1.2 times (120%) the calendar week total return of Microsoft ($MSFT), plus a distribution every week.

This is the part most people miss: $MSFW does not sell covered calls. It gets its Microsoft exposure mostly through a total return swap. Roundhill's holdings file shows about 120% combined Microsoft exposure: a swap worth about 112.6% of net assets plus about 7.5% in actual $MSFT shares, with T-bills and a money market fund behind it. Nothing caps the upside. The weekly payout is set by Roundhill, and the trade-off is a fund that moves 1.2x as hard as Microsoft in both directions, every single week.

  • Issuer: Roundhill Investments
  • Strategy: 1.2x weekly exposure to $MSFT, weekly distributions, no options sold
  • Expense ratio: 1.00% (gross)
  • Net assets: about $28 million (November 2026), small for a fund this well known
  • Distribution rate: 36.46% as of November 13, 2026 (annualizes only the newest payout)
  • 30-day SEC yield: 1.92% as of October 31, 2026

It's one of a big family of single-stock WeeklyPay funds. I ranked them on total return and price decay in the best WeeklyPay ETFs to buy in 2026, and the full Roundhill shelf is in the Roundhill WeeklyPay ETFs distributions and performance guide.

When does $MSFW pay? The weekly schedule

Roundhill runs $MSFW on a tight weekly rhythm: the distribution is declared on Friday (Thursday in some holiday weeks), the ex-date and record date are Monday, and the cash pays on Tuesday. To get a given week's check, you need to own shares before Monday's open, which means buying by Friday's close.

Roundhill's published calendar has the next ones lined up: declared November 20, ex November 23, paid November 24; then November 27, 30 and December 1; then December 4, 7 and 8. For the full list of funds that pay this way, see my list of weekly paying ETFs for 2026.

Why did the payouts shrink? Microsoft itself was hit hard over the same stretch, closing at $352.83 on that same June 25 low, and a 1.2x fund falls faster on the way down. The real test: total return (and the price decay)

Here's what I actually look at before I buy any weekly payer: total return. Share price change plus every distribution. Everything else is marketing.

$MSFW closed its first trading day (July 24, 2025) at about $50.74. It closed at $32.25 on November 13, 2026. That's down about 36%, while it paid out about $15.95 per share in cash along the way (67 paid distributions). That's why our live list shows price decay for $MSFW. And it happened while Microsoft itself finished the period a little higher than where it started.

So $MSFW turned $10,000 into about $9,500 in roughly 16 months if you took the cash. Plain Microsoft stock turned the same $10,000 into about $10,340. Neither one set the world on fire, but the fund built to deliver 1.2x of Microsoft ended up behind Microsoft.

That gap is volatility drag plus the payout. Microsoft dropped hard into June 2026 and then climbed back. A fund that resets 1.2x leverage every week falls further than the stock on the way down, and then it needs a bigger gain just to get back to even. Pay out cash every week on top of that, and the share price struggles to recover. Roundhill's own numbers as of September 30, 2026: -4.77% over one year and -1.94% a year since inception on market price. Our live list shows about -2% since inception on its own methodology. Same conclusion: roughly flat to slightly down.

The other risks: $MSFW is a single-stock fund with 1.2x leverage through swaps, so there's counterparty risk and a big Microsoft drop gets amplified. The 1.00% expense ratio is high. Part of a payout like this can be return of capital, which lowers your cost basis instead of being taxed right away, but also means some of the "income" is the fund handing your money back, so check Roundhill's 19a-1 notices and your 1099. At about $28 million in assets it's also a small fund. And the payout can drop fast, as it did from $0.269 in early June to $0.082 a week later.

Who $MSFW is for (and who it isn't)

  • It can make sense if you're bullish on Microsoft, want leveraged exposure plus weekly cash flow while you wait, and can stomach a fund that fell from about $51 to $23 before bouncing. Think of it as a 1.2x $MSFT position that spins off cash, not a paycheck replacement.
  • It doesn't fit if you need predictable income. A $0.269 week was followed by an $0.082 week in June. If you're budgeting off one check, you'll get burned.
  • Reinvesting changes the math. Plowing the checks back in raises your share count and fights the price decay, but it also raises your leveraged exposure to one stock. Spending every check means watching the position shrink when Microsoft goes nowhere. I'd decide based on how much $MSFT risk you actually want.

For a steadier core, I'd rather build on diversified payers and keep single-stock funds like $MSFW as a small satellite.
