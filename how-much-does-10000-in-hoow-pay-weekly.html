What is $HOOW?

$HOOW is the Roundhill HOOD WeeklyPay ETF. It started trading on June 18, 2025, and it tries to give you 1.2 times (120%) the calendar week total return of Robinhood Markets ($HOOD), plus a distribution every week.

Here's the part most people miss: $HOOW doesn't sell covered calls. It gets its Robinhood exposure mostly through total return swaps. Roundhill's holdings as of November 19, 2026 show Robinhood at about 123.5% of net assets, combining the stock position with the swaps. Nothing caps the upside, and nothing cushions the downside. Roundhill sets the weekly payout with a formula based on Robinhood's performance and implied volatility, and Robinhood is a very volatile stock. That's where the giant checks come from.

  • Issuer: Roundhill Investments
  • Strategy: 1.2x weekly exposure to $HOOD, weekly distributions, no options sold
  • Expense ratio: 0.99% (gross)
  • Net assets: about $83.9 million (November 19, 2026)
  • Distribution rate: 66.13% as of November 19, 2026 (annualizes only the newest payout)
  • 30-day SEC yield: 3.34% as of October 31, 2026

Robinhood doesn't pay a dividend, so $HOOW is one of the few ways to turn a $HOOD position into weekly cash flow. When does $HOOW pay? The weekly schedule

$HOOW runs on the standard Roundhill WeeklyPay rhythm: the distribution is declared on Friday (Thursday in some holiday weeks), the ex-date and record date are Monday, and the cash pays on Tuesday. To get a given week's check, you need to own shares before Monday's open, which means buying by Friday's close.

Roundhill's published calendar has the next ones lined up: declared November 27, ex November 30, paid December 1; then December 4, 7 and 8; then December 11, 14 and 15; then December 18, 21 and 22. For every fund that pays this way, see my list of weekly paying ETFs for 2026.

The real test: total return (and the price decay)

Here's what I actually look at before I buy any weekly payer: total return. Share price change plus every distribution. Everything else is marketing.

$HOOW closed its first trading day (June 18, 2025) at about $52.75. It closed at $13.69 on November 19, 2026. That's down about 74%, while it paid out about $47.68 per share in cash along the way (73 paid distributions). Over the same stretch Robinhood stock went from about $78.35 to $68.65, down about 12%.

This is the most important table in the post. If you bought $HOOW on day one and spent every check, you're up about 16% and you beat Robinhood stock. If you reinvested every check, you're down about 27%, worse than just owning $HOOD. Same fund, same dates, opposite results. Our live list, which counts reinvested distributions, shows the same thing: about -27% since inception.

Why? Because the big checks came early, when the fund was $60 to $85, and reinvesting them bought shares that then fell 80%. Taking the cash locked it in. That's the strange math of a leveraged single-stock income fund on a stock that ran up and then got cut in half.

And the timing matters. Through September 30, 2026, Roundhill's own numbers showed -32.32% over one year but +31.36% a year since inception. Then $HOOD fell from about $112.50 at the end of September to $68.65, about 39%, and $HOOW at 1.2x fell from $26.90 to $13.69, about 49%.

The risks: $HOOW is a single-stock fund with 1.2x leverage through swaps, so there's counterparty risk and every Robinhood drop gets amplified. Roundhill notes the fund could lose all of its value if $HOOD fell about 83% in a single week. The 0.99% expense ratio is high. Roundhill's most recent 19a-1 estimate put the distribution at 100% return of capital, which lowers your cost basis instead of being taxed right away, but also means the "income" is the fund handing your money back, so check your 1099. And the payout can swing hard, from $0.53 to $0.24 to $0.46 in three September weeks.

Who $HOOW is for (and who it isn't)

  • It can make sense if you're bullish on Robinhood, want a bit more than 1x exposure, and plan to take the weekly cash out. Think of it as a 1.2x $HOOD trade that spins off cash, not a paycheck replacement.
  • It doesn't fit if you need predictable income. The check has ranged from $2.46 to $0.12. If you're budgeting off one week, you'll get burned.
  • Be careful with reinvesting. On most income funds I like reinvesting. Here, reinvesting turned a +16% result into -27%, because you kept buying more of a fund that was falling. If you reinvest, you're making a bigger and bigger leveraged bet on one stock. Size it like that.

For a steadier core, I'd rather build on diversified payers and keep single-stock funds like $HOOW as a small satellite. My takeaway

$10,000 in $HOOW pays about $127 this week and has averaged about $205 a week over the last 13 weeks. Since launch it has declared about $47.85 per share, almost 3.5 times today's share price, but the price is down about 74%. A day one $10,000 buy is worth roughly $11,633 if you took the cash, and roughly $7,318 if you reinvested it.

That spread is the whole lesson. The yield on $HOOW is real cash, but most of it is your own money coming back (Roundhill estimates 100% return of capital), and the fund only works if you treat it as a leveraged Robinhood position you're harvesting, not a dividend stock you're compounding.
