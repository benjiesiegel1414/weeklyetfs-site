The Short Answer

Almost no individual stocks pay weekly dividends. Companies pay quarterly, and a handful of REITs pay monthly. The securities that pay every week are weekly dividend ETFs, most of them option income funds. We track 109 of them, updated daily.

Why companies don't pay weekly dividends

A dividend is a decision a company's board makes, and boards don't meet every week.

Every dividend from a regular company has to be declared by its board of directors, with a record date, an ex-dividend date and a payment date. That process lines up with how companies report earnings, which is once a quarter. Paying weekly would mean 52 declarations a year, 52 sets of paperwork and 52 chances to disappoint shareholders if the cash flow dips. No operating business earns money in neat weekly slices, so almost none try.

That is why the normal schedule in the US is quarterly. A smaller group of companies, mostly real estate investment trusts and business development companies, pay monthly because their rent and loan income comes in monthly. Weekly is a different animal altogether.

So what pays weekly? Funds. An option income ETF sells options on a stock or index every day or every week and collects premium as it goes. Because cash comes in constantly, the fund can pass it out on a weekly schedule. Every name in the tables below is one of those funds, and most of them hold or track the stocks people are really searching for.

What to know before buying a weekly payer

  • 📉
    Price decay. Many weekly funds pay out more than their holdings earn, so the share price drifts lower over time. Our Price Decay column flags it. See the lowest price decay weekly ETFs for the ones that have held up.
  • 🧢
    Capped upside. Selling calls trades future gains for income today. In a big rally the stock can leave the fund behind.
  • 🎯
    Single-stock risk. A fund built on one company rises and falls with that company. There is no diversification inside it.
  • ✂️
    Payouts change every week. Distributions are not fixed like a company dividend. They can be cut sharply after a bad week.
  • 🧾
    Taxes. A large share of many weekly payouts is classified as return of capital, which lowers your cost basis instead of being taxed right away. The rest is usually ordinary income, not qualified dividends. Check with a tax professional.

Weekly dividend stocks: questions, answered

Are there any stocks that pay weekly dividends?

Practically none. Ordinary operating companies in the US pay dividends quarterly, and a small group of REITs and business development companies pay monthly. Almost every security that pays every week is a fund, usually an option income ETF, because those funds collect option premium continuously and can pass it through on a weekly schedule. When you search for weekly dividend stocks, the results are almost always weekly paying ETFs.

What is the highest paying weekly dividend stock?

Since weekly payers are funds rather than companies, the highest paying weekly security in our database is $MSTY, the YieldMax MSTR Option Income Strategy ETF, at a distribution rate of about 100%. A rate that high is paid partly out of the fund's own value, so check total return and price decay before treating it as income.

What are the top 10 weekly dividend stocks?

By total return since launch, the strongest weekly payers in our database are $NVDY, $AMDW, $AMDY, $PLTY, $CHPY, $SNOY, $TSMY, $GOOW, $ARMW, $AMZY. By headline yield the list is very different, and several of the highest yielders have lost money overall once price declines are counted.

How much do I need to invest to get $100 a week in dividends?

It depends on the yield. At 10 percent you would need about $52,000, at 20 percent about $26,000, and at 40 percent about $13,000. Higher yields need less money but usually carry more risk of price decay and distribution cuts. Our weekly dividend calculator runs the numbers for any amount.

Can I build weekly income from monthly dividend stocks?

Partly. Monthly payers such as $O, $MAIN, $STAG, $AGNC and $EPR pay on different days of the month, so holding several of them spreads your income across the month. It will not land every single week, which is why many income investors pair monthly stocks with one or two weekly paying ETFs.

Are weekly dividend ETFs safe?

They carry more risk than a typical dividend stock fund. Most use options strategies that cap upside, many pay out more than they earn, and single-stock versions are tied to one volatile company. Lower yielding funds built on broad indexes, such as $XDTE and $WEEK, have been steadier. See our guide to the safest weekly dividend ETFs for a full breakdown.
